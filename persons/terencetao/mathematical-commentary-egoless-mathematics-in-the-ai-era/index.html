AI 时代的无我数学

Karthik C. S., Amit Sahai and Jie Xue 2026-11-02

［本文为 Karthik C. S.、Amit Sahai 和 Jie Xue 的客座文章。这篇博文最初以另一种文件格式撰写，后使用 AI 转换格式。——T.］

现在正是数学家认真思考自己工作目的的好时机。人工智能的发展，以一种异乎寻常的力度把这个问题摆在我们面前。许多讨论关注机器将执行哪些任务，以及哪些能力仍将为人类所独有。但我们的回应还取决于一个更先在的问题：我们如何理解数学工作，又是什么赋予它价值？如果我们的答案已经与成果的产出和个人能力的展示不可分割，那么眼下的不确定性或许揭示了我们对这项工作本身的理解存在问题。

对于数学，大致存在两种观点。一种认为，我们在构建数学的过程中发明数学：我们引入概念、选择公理并发展理论。另一种认为，数学独立于这些活动而存在，我们的任务是发现它。这两种观点并非必须在数学实践的每一个方面都意见相左。我们显然会发展形式体系和论证。更深层的问题是：我们逐渐理解的真理是否因这些行为而存在，还是说，我们的构建使我们得以认识某种不依赖于我们的东西。

发现数学真理听起来或许有些被动，但寻找化石的古生物学家必须决定在哪里发掘、发展技术，并识别不完整遗骸的意义。化石早在调查开始之前就已存在，但找到它们可能需要非凡的想象力。它们预先存在这一事实，为研究者的活动指明了方向。同样，一项数学发现可能需要发明一整套语言和框架，使一种此前未被注意到的关系变得可以理解。发现可能需要极大的创造力，但这并不意味着所发现的真理是个人的创造物。每当我们自己的构建令我们惊讶时，我们都会遇到这一区别。为某个目的引入的定义，在别处揭示了一种意料之外的联系。一个看似能够统摄我们所举例子的猜想，却存在反例。通过不同问题发展出的两种理论，最终被发现表达的是一种共同结构。在每种情形中，我们的思维都遇到了某种并非由它自由决定的东西。我们可以修改定义，也可以放弃猜想，但不能仅凭愿望就让原来的论证变得有效。

柏拉图主义的观点为这种独立性提供了一种自然的解释。按照这一观点，在任何人证明素数有无穷多个之前，素数就已经有无穷多个。数学真理既不等待我们去认识它，也不从我们的认识中获得权威。我们可以选择如何描述一种结构，却不能以同样的方式选择它的性质。这一观点也改变了数学家的位置。我们的智能成为理解一种独立于我们的秩序的途径。这项工作的意义，就在于它与那种秩序的关系。

正确的推导与对其意义的理解之间的区别，同样重要。一个公理系统允许无穷无尽的推演，但能够产生这些推演，本身并不能提供一个值得追求的研究方向。数学命题可以在形式上正确，却并不具有启发性。我们可以引入无数定义，并证明关于它们的无数命题。是什么使某个定义具有启发性？为什么一个定理揭示了某种根本性的东西，而另一个定理只是增加了现有材料？这些问题涉及某项具体研究与它所揭示的原理之间的关系。数学品味在这里有其位置，前提是我们对它的理解不止于个人偏好。当一个理论揭示了原先被分开理解的事物之间的统一性，或使我们认识到某种现象为何发生时，我们便会珍视它。尽管数学真理可能独立于我们而存在，但发现它们可以改变我们的理解，丰富我们对世界的体验。能够进行更长的推理链，本身并不能解释这种理解。推理通过阐明关系并明确其依据，来服务于理解。然而，推理的成功并不意味着一切知识都可以归结为推理。我们能够进行更广泛的推理，也不能决定什么值得知道，或者我们为何追求知识。

数学只是知识的一个领域。并非一切都能用数学来理解，数学成就也不是智识生活的最终目的。然而，我们越来越多地通过论文、引用、奖项和其他绩效指标来衡量智识生活。这些衡量方式虽有用，但当它们定义了工作本身的价值时，就会带来问题。我们开始把数学家视为一组生产能力的集合，把数学仅仅视为他们的产出。从这个角度看，对被 AI 取代的焦虑是自然的：一台更擅长执行这些任务的机器，似乎会威胁我们的价值。但问题在 AI 到来之前就已经开始了。我们早已开始用适合描述机器的方式来理解自己。AI 只是暴露了这种观点的后果。

我们的回应不能仅仅是退守到创造力、直觉或品味等独属于人类的能力之中。如果我们主要因为这些能力使我们相对于机器具有优势而珍视它们，那么我们仍然被困在同一种关于智识价值的观念中。为什么我们所从事的追求，其价值应当取决于我们比另一个人或另一种事物做得更好？

数学提供了一个重新审视这一整套取向的机会。数学真理不会因为表达它的人卓尔不群而变得更加真实。在理解一个证明时，我们使自己的思维符合一种关系，而这种关系的有效性并不属于我们。我们抵达理解的具体路径，可能带有个人特性的印记。但被理解的内容超越了这种个人特性，为他人所能把握。我们可以让这种体验松动一种信念：知识的最高价值在于它是一项可归功于我们自己的成就。

无我之数学，始于认真对待这种可能性。此时，谦逊便不再只是对自己的成就保持谦虚，或在分配功劳时慷慨大方。它关乎我们从什么立场出发去追求知识。我们可以为了服务既有目的而寻求真理，也可以让我们的发现改变这些目的本身。如果我们接近真理时附加了一个条件，要求发现真理必须维护我们自身的重要性，那么我们就已经让真理从属于自己。完成这种重新排序可能很艰难，因为智识上的精深可能为那些恰恰阻碍我们理解的执着，提供精巧复杂的辩护。重要的是，我们的能力是否始终对真理负责，包括真理要求我们重新考虑一直以来运用这些能力所服务的目的之时。

从这个角度看，通过 AI 取得数学发现的可能性，不必成为我们因自身使命而悲伤的缘由。一个真理变得可以接近；它的价值不会因为我们不是发现者而减少。我们不必立刻把这件事变成对我们相对重要性的评判。探索可以继续，无须每一次进展都证明探索者自身的价值。事实上，无论竞争性的主张来自另一位数学家还是一台机器，对这种自我证明的要求一直都是探索的负担。

因此，摆在我们面前的机会，比重新安排数学劳动更加深刻。它邀请我们重新思考是什么赋予智识生活以价值，并让对真理的追求不仅塑造我们的工作，也塑造我们面对生活的方式。

这听起来或许像是在要求我们所有人都成为圣人。就它要求我们增进德性、放下对自身重要性的执着而言，这种印象与实情相差不远。但培养德性并不是为了获得他人的赞赏。即使在工作中，它也是真正喜悦所必需的。如果解决难题或赢得奖项带来的满足，建立在我们相对于他人的地位上，那么这种满足可能稍纵即逝。理解的喜悦则不同。某种此前晦暗不明的东西变得清晰，我们认识到一种美，而这种美不会因为别人先发现它就有所减损。我们可以分享这种喜悦，而不剥夺任何人的喜悦。德性的增长，让我们能够更充分地体验它，不再需要确立自身的重要性。

这一观点也可能有助于调和数学共同体内部关于 AI 的两种立场。有些人乐于，甚至热衷于使用 AI，但希望为它的使用制定适当的流程和原则。另一些人则倾向于完全不使用 AI。两种立场都有其可取之处。无我之数学通过引入一种更高层次的考量，为调和二者提供了一条道路：无论我们选择如何追求数学真理，这两种方式都可以体现同一种根本承诺。我们在这里的目标不是解决具体的政策问题，而是发展一种更高层次的视角，用以指导我们的审议，并最终指导我们的行动。

这样看来，我们无须害怕 AI 可能在数学中取得的成就。数学在我们所有人出生之前就已美丽，在我们离去很久之后也仍将美丽。它的价值不取决于我们的成就，我们理解它时的喜悦也不必取决于我们是不是作出发现的人。我们的目的，建立在我们愿意让真理支配我们的追求，以及我们将成为什么样的人之上。

我们这些本文的作者，距离达到这样的无我境界还很遥远。尽管如此，我们仍将它视为自己愿意努力趋近的理想。

最后，我们以一个思想实验作结。据传，拉马努金曾说：“一个方程对我而言没有意义，除非它表达了上帝的一个思想。”（Robert Kanigel，《知无涯者》，1992）如果他今天仍然在世，见证了 AI 在数学中的进展，他会因为这些进展对自己作为数学家的目的可能意味着什么而感到不安吗？

This is a good time for mathematicians to think carefully about the purpose of their work. The development of artificial intelligence brings that question before us with unusual force. Much of the discussion concerns which tasks machines will perform and which abilities will remain distinctively human. But our response also depends on a prior question: what do we understand mathematical work to be, and what gives it its worth? If our answer has become inseparable from the production of results and the demonstration of individual ability, the present uncertainty may reveal a difficulty in our conception of the work itself.

There are broadly two perspectives on mathematics. According to one, we invent it as we construct it: we introduce concepts, choose axioms, and develop theories. According to the other, mathematics exists independently of these activities, and our task is to discover it. These perspectives need not disagree about every aspect of mathematical practice. We plainly develop formalism and arguments. The deeper question is whether the truths we come to understand owe their existence to these acts, or whether our constructions enable us to recognize something that does not depend on us.

While discovery of mathematical truth can sound passive, a paleontologist searching for fossils must decide where to excavate, develop techniques, and recognize the significance of incomplete remains. The fossils existed long before the investigation, but finding them may require extraordinary imagination. Their prior existence gives direction to the investigator’s activity. Similarly, a mathematical discovery may require the invention of an entire language and framework through which a previously unnoticed relationship becomes intelligible. The discovery may require great creativity, but that does not make the truth discovered a creation of the individual. We encounter this distinction whenever our constructions surprise us. A definition introduced for one purpose reveals an unexpected connection elsewhere. A conjecture that seemed to organize our examples admits a counterexample. Two theories developed through different questions turn out to express a common structure. In each case, our thought encounters something it did not freely determine. We can revise our definitions or abandon our conjecture, but we cannot make the original argument valid merely by wishing it so.

The Platonic perspective gives a natural account of this independence. On this view, there were infinitely many primes before anyone proved that fact. Mathematical truth neither waits for our recognition nor receives its authority from it. We may choose how to describe a structure, but we do not choose its properties in the same way. This perspective also changes the place of the mathematician. Our intelligence becomes a means through which an order independent of us can be understood. The significance of the work lies in its relation to that order.

The distinction between a correct derivation and an understanding of its significance is equally important. An axiomatic system permits indefinitely many deductions, but the ability to produce them does not by itself supply a worthy direction of inquiry. Mathematical statements may be formally correct without being enlightening. We can introduce countless definitions and prove countless statements about them. What makes one definition illuminating? Why does one theorem disclose something fundamental while another merely adds to the available material? These questions concern the relation of a particular investigation to the principles it brings into view. Mathematical taste has a place here, provided we understand it as more than individual preference. We value a theory when it reveals a unity among things previously understood separately, or enables us to recognize why a phenomenon occurs. Although mathematical truths may exist independently of us, their discovery can transform our understanding and enrich our experience of the world. The ability to carry out longer chains of reasoning does not by itself explain such understanding. Reasoning serves understanding by articulating relationships and making their justification explicit. However, the success of reasoning does not mean that all knowledge can be reduced to it. Nor does our ability to reason more extensively determine what is worth knowing or why we seek knowledge.

Mathematics is only one field of knowledge. Not everything can be understood mathematically, nor is mathematical achievement the ultimate purpose of intellectual life. Yet we increasingly measure intellectual life through publications, citations, awards, and other indicators of performance. While useful, these measures become problematic when they define the worth of the work itself. We begin to see mathematicians as collections of productive abilities, and mathematics as merely their output. From this perspective, anxiety about being replaced by AI is natural: a machine that performs these tasks better appears to threaten our worth. But the problem began before AI arrived. We had already come to understand ourselves in terms suited to machines. AI merely exposes the consequences of this view.

The response cannot simply be to seek refuge in distinctively human abilities such as creativity, intuition, or taste. If we value these abilities primarily because they give us an advantage over machines, we remain trapped in the same conception of intellectual worth. Why should the value of our pursuit depend on being better at it than someone or something else?

Mathematics offers an opportunity to reconsider this whole orientation. Its truths do not become more true through the distinction of the person who expresses them. In understanding a proof, we bring our thought into conformity with a relationship whose validity does not belong to us. The particular path by which we arrive may bear the marks of our individuality. What is understood is available beyond that individuality. We can allow this experience to loosen the belief that knowledge has its highest value as an achievement attributable to ourselves.

Egoless mathematics begins with taking that possibility seriously. Humility then reaches beyond modesty about our accomplishments or generosity in sharing credit. It concerns the place from which we pursue knowledge. We may seek truth to serve our existing purposes, or allow what we discover to change those purposes themselves. If we approach truth on the condition that its discovery must preserve our importance, we have already made it subordinate to ourselves. Accomplishing this reordering can be demanding, as intellectual sophistication can furnish elaborate defenses of the very attachments that prevent us from understanding. What matters is whether our abilities remain answerable to truth, including when it requires us to reconsider what we have been using those abilities for.

From this perspective, the possibility of mathematical discoveries through AI need not be a source of sadness about our purpose. A truth has become accessible; its worth is not diminished by the absence of our authorship. We need not immediately turn the event into a judgment about our relative importance. The search can continue without requiring every advance to vindicate the seeker. Indeed, the demand for such vindication has always burdened the pursuit, whether the competing claim came from another mathematician or from a machine.

The opportunity before us is therefore more substantial than a rearrangement of mathematical labor. It invites us to reconsider what gives an intellectual life its worth, and to let the pursuit of truth shape not only our work but also how we approach life.

This may sound like asking us all to become saints. Insofar as it asks us to grow in virtue and relinquish our attachment to our own importance, that impression is not far from the truth. But virtue is not something to cultivate for the admiration of others. It is necessary for genuine joy, even in our work. The satisfaction of solving a difficult problem or winning a prize can be fleeting when it rests on our standing relative to others. The joy of understanding is different. Something previously obscure becomes clear, and we recognize a beauty that does not diminish when someone else discovers it first. We can share this joy without depriving anyone of it. Growing in virtue allows us to experience it more fully, free from the need to establish our own importance.

This perspective may also help reconcile two positions within the mathematical community regarding AI. Some are comfortable with or even enthusiastic about using AI, but wish to develop appropriate processes and principles for its use. Others prefer not to use AI at all. Both positions have their merits. Egoless mathematics offers a way of reconciling them by introducing a higher consideration: both approaches can reflect the same underlying commitment to mathematical truth, regardless of how we choose to pursue it. Our aim here is not to settle particular questions of policy, but to develop a higher-level perspective that can guide our deliberations and ultimately our actions.

Seen in this light, we need not be afraid of what AI may achieve in mathematics. Mathematics was beautiful before any of us were born, and it will remain beautiful long after we are gone. Its worth does not depend on our accomplishments, nor does our joy in understanding it have to depend on being the ones who make the discoveries. Our purpose rests on our willingness to let truth govern what we seek and what we become.

We, the authors of this essay, are still far from having attained such egolessness. Nevertheless, we recognize it as an ideal toward which we wish to strive.

We conclude with a thought experiment. Ramanujan is reported to have said, “An equation has no meaning for me unless it expresses a thought of God” (Robert Kanigel, The Man Who Knew Infinity, 1992). If he were alive today, witnessing the advances of AI in mathematics, would he be perturbed by what they might mean for his purpose as a mathematician?
