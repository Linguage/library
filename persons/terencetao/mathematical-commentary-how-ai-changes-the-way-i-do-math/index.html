AI 如何改变、又如何不改变我做数学的方式

Rachel Webb 2026-09-29

[本文为 Rachel Webb 撰写的客座文章。这篇博文最初以另一种文件格式写成，后使用 AI 转换。——T.]

我做数学的方式曾经经历过一次巨变，那是在我读博的中途。当时，我和一群研究生及教师参加一个研讨班，一起开发针对不同学科的新写作课程。主持人提出了这样一个问题：

在你的学科里，怎样才算写得好？

那时和现在一样，我用写作来代指实践。作为一个博士读到半程的研究生，我很快就有了答案：好的数学写作，也就是好的数学，应当清晰、正确。其他研讨班成员的回答与我的大不相同，这让我颇感意外。他们更有经验，但也来自其他学科：好的写作要说出有意思的东西。

这番讨论让我明白了做研究意味着什么。做研究，就是寻找值得一说的、有意思的东西。（当然，一旦找到了，就必须清晰、正确地把它表达出来。）在数学中，寻找有意思的东西，往往由我们对理解的追求所驱动，正如 Thurston 所指出的那样，此后也有许多人赞同这一点。但我认为，数学研究过程有创造性的一面，并不能完全归结于这种追求。相比之下，创造值得一说的、有意思的东西，就像写一部小说：小说人物既受到人类经验的约束，又以某种方式呈现出来，对人类经验作出评论。数学小说中的人物是定义，故事正文是引理和定理；它们受到真理的约束，但也可以经过有策略或有艺术性的选择，以捕捉真理的某些侧面。

从抽象的层面说，这就是我目前从事数学研究的方式：没错，我努力从根本上理解正在发生的事，但我不认为理解只有唯一一种方式。我也努力把自己确实理解的东西拿来——不可避免地，这些东西只是我想理解的现象中的一部分——编织成一段既优美、又能让数学同行觉得有意思的叙事。具体说来，我发现，数学语境中的“趣味”常常来自应用，无论是应用于现实世界，还是应用于其他数学领域。它也来自与备受瞩目的开放问题的接近程度，这些问题是数学讨论的中心。

我不觉得大语言模型（LLM）会让这种研究方式发生多大变化，但我预计，它们会彻底改变我践行这种方式的过程。

实践过程之所以改变，是因为现在我可以借助一台读过所有书、知道许多标准引理是怎么回事的机器。这极大地加快了研究进程，也使我数学幻想中的一些国度变成了可以真正开始探索的世界（Antieau 说，要做更大的梦）。当然，选择州际公路而不是支路，自有其得失；但在旅程的任何一段，我都可以选择走哪条路。稍后我会再谈到这个想法。

我的研究方式没有改变，关键在于，我并不确信大语言模型的出现改变了我衡量数学“趣味”的标准。如果我们过去认为，一篇论文只要以某种方式讨论了一个千禧年难题（或类似问题），就算有意思，那么，当这个问题有了解答时，我们就必须减轻对它的兴趣吗？当然可以有许多种解答，甚至还有许多尚不足以构成完整解答的理解片段。它们全都很有意思。我们或许已经知道怎样到达那座红色城市，但仍可以继续绘制周边地形的地图；由于毗邻大都市而产生的经济和战略机遇，如今使这些地图愈加有价值。

我已经谈了一些自己将如何继续做数学研究，却还没有讨论为什么要做：假如大语言模型能够（姑且作这样的假设）产出比我们所能创造的数学更有意思的数学，人类还有什么理由做数学？我相信有经济方面的理由，但这里不讨论它们（Sahai 和 Strogatz、Townsend 都有一些想法）。我想提出的是两个人文层面的理由。它们都并非数学所独有，正如数学也不是唯一一种实践方式受到 AI 出现影响的人类活动。

人类做数学的第一个理由是，数学对我们每个人来说都有意思。我喜欢做数学，所以即使机器做得更好，我也会继续做。这里存在一种威胁：大语言模型可能诱使我们更重视知道答案，而不是理解解法，从而夺走做数学的乐趣。这种诱惑必须抵制。我应该只以能让我更享受数学研究、并让我在获得智慧的同时也获得理解（《箴言》4:7）的方式使用 AI。这可能需要尝试。事实上，我和其他人早已在作类似的选择：只在技术对我们个人有帮助的时候使用它，而不是每逢这样做在经济上“正确”时就使用。比如，我每周都会说服孩子们步行往返七英里去教堂，尽管我们本可以开车。这一来一回要花上远不止两小时，但共处与共苦让家人之间增加了理解，这就值得。

人类做数学的第二个理由是，数学能同时引起多个人的共同兴趣，并由此形成社群。从某种意义上说，这正是那些来自其他学科的研讨班成员所说的“好的写作有意思”的含义：他们的意思是，好的写作能让别人觉得有意思，因而成为一场更广泛对话的一部分。数学研究是一种把人们聚到一起的工具，在师生关系中、在合作中、在会议上，以及在院系的综合学术报告会和茶歇时间里，都是如此。同样，AI 可能会使被抢先发表成果（的担忧）更为常见，或者仅仅因为请教机器比请教同事更方便，而削弱这些社会纽带。但可能并不自动意味着一定会。借用 Wendell Berry 的话，愿 AI 的时代成为我们认识数学邻人的时代。“朋友们，每天都做点无法计算的事。”

The way I do math experienced a great upheaval once before, halfway through my PhD. I was in a seminar with a group of graduate students and faculty developing new discipline-specific writing courses. The facilitator asked the question,

What does it mean to write well in your discipline?

Then, as now, I use writing as a proxy for doing. As a mid-career grad student, I had a quick answer to this question: good mathematical writing, hence good mathematics, is clear and correct. I was surprised at the dissimilarity between my answer and the answer of the other seminarians (who were more experienced but also other-disciplined): good writing says something interesting.

This discussion unlocked for me the meaning of doing research. Doing research is searching for something interesting to say. (Of course, once you find it, you have to say it in a way that is clear and correct.) In mathematics, the search for something interesting is often driven by our search for understanding, as noted by Thurston and endorsed by many since. But I think the math research process has a creative aspect that is not completely captured by this search. By contrast, the task of making something interesting to say is like writing a novel whose characters are at once subject to constraints of human experience but also presented in a way that comments on human experience. The characters in mathematical novels are definitions, the story text is the lemmas and theorems; these are constrained by truth, but can also be chosen strategically or artistically to capture certain facets of truth.

This is, abstractly, how I currently approach math research: I seek to understand what is happening at a fundamental level, yes, but I don’t think there is a unique way to understand. I also seek to take the things I do understand—inevitably, these are just a subset of the phenomena I would like to understand—and craft a narrative from them that is beautiful and interesting to my fellow mathematicians. Concretely, I find that “interest” in a mathematical context often derives from applications, either to the real world, or to other math. It also derives from proximity to high-profile open problems that serve as centers of mathematical conversations.

I don’t see LLMs as changing that approach much, but I expect they will drastically affect how I execute it.

The execution changes because now I have access to a machine that has read all the books and knows how many standard lemmas go. This speeds up the research process immensely and turns some of my lands of mathematical fantasy into worlds I can realistically start exploring (dream bigger dreams, says Antieau). Of course, taking the interstate instead of the side roads has its tradeoffs, but for any given leg of the journey I can choose which route to take. I will return to this idea in a moment.

My approach to research does not change because critically, I’m not convinced that the advent of LLMs changes my metric for mathematical “interest.” If we have historically regarded a paper as interesting if it comments in some way on a Millennium (or similar) problem, need we lessen our interest if the problem has a solution? Certainly there are many solutions, even many shards of understanding that do not constitute full solutions. These are all very interesting. We might know how to get to the red city, but we can continue to map out the surrounding terrain, now all the more valuable for the economic and strategic opportunities arising from metropolitan proximity.

I have said something about how I will continue to do math research, but I have not discussed why: what reasons do humans have to do math, if LLMs are capable (hypothetically, say) of producing math that is even more interesting than the math we can create? I believe that there are economic reasons, but I will not discuss those (both Sahai and Strogatz-Townsend have some thoughts). Instead I present two humanistic reasons. Neither of these is unique to math, just as math is not the unique human practice whose execution is affected by the advent of AI.

The first reason for humans to do math is that math is interesting to us individually. I enjoy doing math, so I will keep doing it, even if machines are better at it. There is a threat that LLMs will take the fun out of doing math by tempting us towards knowing the answer over understanding the solution. The temptation must be resisted. I should use AI only in ways that make math research more enjoyable for me and allow me to get understanding along with my wisdom (Proverbs 4:7). This may require experimentation. In fact, I and others already make analogous choices to use technology only when it is helpful to us personally and not every time it is economically “correct.” For example, I persuade my kids every week to walk seven miles round trip to church, even though we could drive. The commute costs well over two hours, but the increase in understanding between family members from the shared time and suffering is worth it.

The second reason for humans to do math is that math holds shared interest for multiple people at once, and in this way it creates communities. In some sense, this is what the other-disciplined seminarians meant when they said good writing is interesting: they meant that good writing is interesting to other people and hence is a piece of a larger conversation. Math research is a tool for drawing people together, in student-teacher relationships, in collaborations, at conferences, and at department colloquia and tea-times. Again, AI could weaken these social bonds by making (fear of) scooping more common, or just by making it easier to ask a machine than a colleague. But could does not automatically imply will. To quote Wendell Berry, may the age of AI be the age of knowing our mathematical neighbor. “Friends, every day do something that won’t compute.”
